# U1.6 Rational Exponents

## Learning Objectives
- Use rational exponents to solve problems.
- Understand the definition and concept behind a rational exponent.
- Convert seamlessly between radical expressions and expressions with rational exponents.

## What is a Rational Exponent?
A **rational exponent** is simply a rational number used as an exponent. 

First, let's recall the definition of a rational number:
> [!NOTE] Rational Number
> A **rational number** is any number that can be expressed as a fraction $\frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers, and $b \neq 0$.

When we see a standard integer exponent like in $x^3$, it indicates how many times the base $x$ should be multiplied by itself ($x \cdot x \cdot x$). However, what does it mean to multiply a number $\frac{1}{2}$ times? 

Let's look at a mathematical justification to understand rational exponents.
Suppose we have the equation $(a^n)^2 = a$. Let's solve for $n$.
By the power of a power rule, we multiply the exponents:
$$(a^n)^2 = a^{2n}$$
Therefore, substituting this back into our original equation gives:
$$a^{2n} = a^1$$
Since the bases are equal ($a = a$), their exponents must also be equal:
$$2n = 1$$
$$n = \frac{1}{2}$$
Therefore, raising a number to the power of $\frac{1}{2}$ is the exact same operation as taking the square root of that number, because $(a^{\frac{1}{2}})^2 = a^{\frac{2}{2}} = a^1 = a$. 
Thus, $a^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt[2]{a^1} = \sqrt{a}$.

> [!TIP]
> Rational exponents are just another way to represent roots. Multiplying a number $\frac{1}{2}$ times is equivalent to taking the square root of the number. Similar reasoning shows that $x^{\frac{1}{3}} = \sqrt[3]{x}$, $x^{\frac{1}{4}} = \sqrt[4]{x}$, and so on.

*Note on Integers:* Although integer exponents (like $5$) can be written as rational numbers with a denominator of $1$ (like $\frac{5}{1}$), they are typically not considered rational exponents in this context, because they don't involve taking a root.

## Converting Between Rational Exponents and Radicals

Any radical expression can be converted to an exponential expression with a rational exponent and vice-versa. 
Previously, we established that $\sqrt{x} = x^{\frac{1}{2}}$, $\sqrt[3]{x} = x^{\frac{1}{3}}$, and $\sqrt[4]{x} = x^{\frac{1}{4}}$. 

Notice that the radicands for these simple expressions have an "invisible" exponent of $1$:
$$ \sqrt{x^1} = \sqrt[2]{x^1} = x^{\frac{1}{2}} $$
$$ \sqrt[3]{x^1} = x^{\frac{1}{3}} $$
$$ \sqrt[4]{x^1} = x^{\frac{1}{4}} $$

### The General Rule

The general formula for converting any radical expression into an exponential expression (and vice-versa) is:

> [!IMPORTANT] The Rational Exponent Rule
> $$ m^{\frac{n}{r}} = \sqrt[r]{m^n} $$
> - **The Base:** The base of the rational exponent ($m$) becomes the base of the radicand.
> - **The Numerator:** The numerator of the rational exponent ($n$) becomes the exponent of the radicand.
> - **The Denominator:** The denominator of the rational exponent ($r$) becomes the index of the radical.

### Examples

**Example 1: Converting a Radical to an Exponential Expression**
Convert $\sqrt[3]{x^2}$ into an exponential expression.
1. The base of the radicand, $x$, remains the base of the rational exponent.
2. The exponent of the radicand is $2$. This becomes the numerator of the rational exponent.
3. The index of the radical is $3$. This becomes the denominator of the rational exponent.

**Result:** $x^{\frac{2}{3}}$

**Example 2: Converting an Exponential Expression to a Radical**
Convert $x^{\frac{8}{5}}$ into a radical expression.
1. The base of the rational exponent, $x$, becomes the base of the radical.
2. The numerator of the rational exponent is $8$. This becomes the exponent of the radicand.
3. The denominator of the rational exponent is $5$. This becomes the index of the radical.

**Result:** $\sqrt[5]{x^8}$

## Practice Problems

Here are some additional examples demonstrating the conversion process in both directions:

1. **Express $300^{\frac{1}{2}}$ as a radical.**
   - The denominator is 2 (square root).
   - The numerator is 1.
   - **Result:** $\sqrt{300}$

2. **Express $(x - 1)^{\frac{3}{4}}$ as a radical.**
   - The denominator is 4 (fourth root).
   - The numerator is 3.
   - **Result:** $\sqrt[4]{(x - 1)^3}$

3. **What exponential expression is equivalent to $\sqrt[4]{x^3}$?**
   - The index is 4 (denominator).
   - The exponent is 3 (numerator).
   - **Result:** $x^{\frac{3}{4}}$

4. **Express $x^{\frac{6}{7}}$ as a radical.**
   - The denominator is 7 (seventh root).
   - The numerator is 6.
   - **Result:** $\sqrt[7]{x^6}$

## Conclusion
Understanding rational exponents and square roots is essential for manipulating algebraic expressions. Being able to easily switch between radical and exponential notation simplifies operations and will be continuously used in solving complex problems and formulating algebraic equations.
